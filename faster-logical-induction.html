Logical Induction, §5, made efficient

Faster Logical Induction

The market-of-traders algorithm from Garrabrant et al.'s Logical Induction, rebuilt so it runs in this page. It keeps the paper's guarantee: no efficiently computable trader can exploit its prices. Every exponential step is gone except one search, and that one is PPAD-hard in general.

The paper pictures a reasoner as someone filling in a spreadsheet, one column per day, with a row for every sentence. Here each cell is that day's price. The atoms a0 to a7 have a hidden truth that the deductive process reveals one by one, and two disjunctions leak out earlier. The row fₙ holds a brand-new sentence every day, which an informed trader knows and a stubborn trader disputes. The row χₙ is a liar sentence, true exactly when its own price that day is below ½; its price settles at ½. Two traders, a "rotator" and an "expander", make the price-finding problem non-monotone on purpose. Another buys any combination that the proved facts already settle, which is what moves the last two combination rows.

How the algorithm works

A trader looks at today's prices and orders a trade: so many shares of each sentence, chosen by a continuous formula of the prices. A trader exploits the market if its worth, judged in every world still consistent with what has been proved, is bounded below but unbounded above. A logical inductor is a market that no polynomial-time trader exploits.

The paper builds one, LIA, from three pieces and one recursion:

\[ \mathbb P_n \;=\; \mathrm{MarketMaker}_n\big(\mathrm{TradingFirm}_n(\mathbb P_{<n}),\ \mathbb P_{<n}\big). \]

Budgeter scales a trader down so it never loses more than a budget \(b\). TradingFirm adds up every trader at every budget with prior weights, giving one supertrader. MarketMaker then finds prices at which that supertrader can make almost nothing today, in any world. That last step is a fixed-point problem, solvable because trades are continuous in prices.

As written, every piece is brute force. MarketMaker enumerates all rational price vectors and checks each against all \(2^d\) truth assignments. Budgeter re-checks every past day for every budget over \(2^{|S'|}\) worlds, where \(S'\) is every sentence the trader has ever touched. TradingFirm sums over a budget bound \(C_n\) that can be doubly exponential.

Lemma 1: the world check collapses

In the paper a "world" is any truth assignment, consistent or not. A trade's value in a world is linear and coordinate-separable, so the best world can be read off sentence by sentence:

\[ \max_{\mathbb W}\ \mathbb W\big(T(p)\big)\;=\;\sum_{\phi}\Big(T_\phi(p)^{+}\,(1-p_\phi)\;+\;T_\phi(p)^{-}\,p_\phi\Big). \]

The right side is zero exactly at the paper's fixed points, so MarketMaker becomes: find \(p\in[0,1]^d\) where this sum is at most \(\varepsilon_n\). Try it on random trades:

Budgets without enumeration

For trader \(k\) with raw holdings \(H^k_{n-1}\) and today's trade \(S^k_n(p)\), the budgeted copy at budget \(b\) trades \(\alpha\cdot S^k_n(p)\) with

\[ \alpha^{k,b}_n(p)=\max\Big\{\alpha\in[0,1]:\ \min_{\mathbb W\in \mathrm{PC}(D_n)}\big[\,b+\mathbb W(H^k_{n-1})+\alpha\,\mathbb W(S^k_n(p))\,\big]\ge 0\Big\}, \]

which is the paper's own scaling factor. The function inside is concave and piecewise linear in \(\alpha\), so Newton's method lands on the answer in a few steps. Each step is one linear minimisation over worlds consistent with \(D_n\). The only change is a margin: a copy stays active while the trader's worst loss so far is at most \(b/2\), not \(b\). That keeps \(\alpha\) Lipschitz.

The firm then weighs trader \(k\) at budget \(2^j\) by

\[ \frac{1}{k(k+1)}\cdot u_j,\qquad u_j=\frac{2^{-j}}{(j+1)^2}-\frac{2^{-j-1}}{(j+2)^2}. \]

The tail \(\sum_{j\ge J}u_j=2^{-J}/(J+1)^2\) is an exact rational number, so the infinite sum over budgets is computed exactly with a handful of terms.

Why it is still a logical inductor

Theorem. For every deductive process \(D\), the prices produced by LIA-fast satisfy the logical induction criterion relative to \(D\). So they inherit every property proved in §4 of the paper: convergence, coherence, provability induction, calibration, non-dogmatism, self-trust.

The argument follows the paper, step by step:

  1. A budgeted copy never loses more than \(b\) in any world consistent with \(D_n\). A trader whose losses stay below \(b/2\) passes through its copy unchanged.
  2. Suppose an efficient trader exploits the market with losses below \(b_0\). Its copy at budget \(2^j\ge 2b_0\) is the trader itself. Every other copy is bounded below by its budget, and the weighted budgets sum to \(B=\tfrac{1+\zeta(2)}{2}\). So the firm also exploits the market: worth at least \(-B\), unbounded above.
  3. MarketMaker picks prices at which the firm's best possible profit today is at most \(\varepsilon_n\), in every world. Lemma 1 makes that cheap to check.
  4. Summing over days, the firm's profit never exceeds \(\sum_n\varepsilon_n<\infty\). That contradicts step 2, so no efficient trader exploits the market.

The ledger above checks step 4 as it runs: the stat "Firm's best profit so far" is \(\sum_n \max_{\mathbb W}\mathbb W(TF_n)\), and it stays under \(\sum_n\varepsilon_n\).

Learning from a trader late in the list

A logical inductor enumerates every polynomial-time trader. The prior weight on trader \(k\) bounds how much that trader can take from the market before its knowledge is priced in. If its plausible losses never exceed \(b_0\), then in every consistent world

\[ \mathbb W(H^k_n)\;\le\;(E+B)\,k(k+1)\cdot 8b_0\big(\log_2(4b_0)+1\big)^2\qquad\text{instead of}\qquad 3\cdot 2^{\,k+\lfloor b_0\rfloor+1}. \]

In a standard enumeration, \(k\) grows exponentially with the length of the trader's program. So the paper's bound is doubly exponential in program length, and the new one is singly exponential. What stays hard

One step keeps an exponential worst case: finding prices. That is not an artefact of the method. Any price-finding routine also finds approximate Brouwer fixed points. Give it the one-trader strategy \(T_f(p)=\sum_i (f_i(p)-p_i)(\phi_i-\phi_i^{*n})\) for a map \(f:[0,1]^d\to[0,1]^d\), and

\[ \mathrm{gap}_{T_f}(p)\;\ge\;\lVert f(p)-p\rVert_2^2 . \]
  • Every gate of Rubinstein's generalized circuits is an expressible feature. So the price-finding step is PPAD-hard even at a constant tolerance, on general inputs.
  • By Chen and Deng's query bound, any black-box method needs on the order of \((M/(d^2\sqrt\varepsilon))^{d-1}\) evaluations in dimension \(d\).
  • The exact budget test is coNP-hard: with nothing yet proved, it decides whether a formula is a tautology. LIA-fast calls an oracle polynomially many times instead of enumerating worlds, so a SAT solver can take the load.

These limits are about the subproblems on general inputs. Whether every logical inductor must pay them is open. In practice the verified heuristics found prices on every day of every scenario here. On deliberately hard random instances, they solve all of them up to 5 sentences and about two thirds at 20 to 40.
